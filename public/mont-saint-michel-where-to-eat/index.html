Where to Eat at Mont-Saint-Michel: the Mère Poulard Omelette, Crêperies and Dinner

What's worth trying, where to eat on the island and on the mainland, and how not to miss dinner once the day crowds have gone.

By EugeneUpdated November 20266-minute read
Get the island audio guide The Mère Poulard omelette

Mont-Saint-Michel has one famous dish, a dozen crêperies and plenty of cafés along a single narrow street — and almost all of them are geared to the daytime crowds. You can eat well here, but it helps to know what you're paying for: the legend, the view, or simply the fact that you're on the island. Here's what to try, where to eat on the island and on the mainland, and how not to miss dinner in the evening.

It's easier to fit food around your walk than the other way round. The self-guided TouringBee island audio guide is a route of about 2 hours that you start whenever you like: before lunch, after the omelette, or towards evening when the village empties.

In short

  • The famous Mère Poulard omelette is an experience and a piece of history, not a cheap lunch; check the price on the current menu.
  • The simplest option on the island is a crêperie on the Grande Rue: savoury galettes made with buckwheat and sweet crêpes.
  • Local produce to look for on menus: salt-meadow lamb and mussels from the bay — both have a protected AOP name.
  • On the mainland, by the car park (La Caserne), there's more choice and it's often calmer.
  • Book dinner ahead: travellers often note that far fewer places are open on the island in the evening.

The Mère Poulard omelette: what it is, and is it worth it?

The island's best-known dish dates from the late 19th century, when Annette Poulard ran an inn here for pilgrims and travellers. Her omelette — fluffy, whisked almost to a foam and cooked over an open fire in copper pans — became famous, and "Mère Poulard" herself became part of the mount's history. The restaurant bearing her name still stands at the entrance to the village; the eggs are whisked to a rhythm you can hear from the street, and that's part of the show.

To be straight about it: this is an expensive omelette. Prices for a single omelette and for set menus vary noticeably between sources — from several tens of euros for an omelette to more for a tasting menu — so check the current menu on the restaurant's website before you go. Many travellers describe it as an experience you pay for once rather than the best value for a filling meal. If you'd like the taste without a full meal, the famous Mère Poulard biscuits are a good compromise: they're sold on the island and far beyond it, and they're a classic souvenir.

What to try besides the omelette

  • Galettes and crêpes. Savoury buckwheat galettes and sweet crêpes are the easiest and most predictable lunch on the island; there are several crêperies on the Grande Rue.
  • Salt-meadow lamb (agneau de pré-salé). The lambs graze on the meadows around the bay that the sea floods, and the meat is distinctive. Look for "Prés-salés du Mont-Saint-Michel AOP" on the menu.
  • Mussels from the bay. Mussels grown on stakes (bouchot) in the bay were the first shellfish in France to receive an AOP. The season is limited, so they're not on menus all year round.
  • Cancale oysters. If you're heading on along the coast, Cancale is about half an hour to an hour away and known for its oysters (more in our Mont-Saint-Michel and Saint-Malo in one day guide).
  • Cider and desserts. In Normandy and Brittany, cider is the traditional drink with galettes.

Where to eat: island or mainland

On the island. The upside is the atmosphere and being inside the walls already. The downsides are the lunchtime crowds, tourist prices and limited choice in the evening. The best tables, with a view over the bay, go first.

On the mainland, by the car park (La Caserne and around). This is where the hotels and restaurants for overnight visitors are; it's usually calmer, and some terraces look out at the Mont. If you're staying on the mainland or heading back to the car, it's often the most convenient place for dinner (see our where-to-stay guide).

A small money tip: carry a little cash — payment terms can vary from place to place.

When to eat: lunch and dinner

Lunch. From the middle of the day to early evening the Grande Rue is at its busiest — the same peak hits the crêperies and cafés. If you can, eat a little earlier or later than the usual lunch hour: the queue is shorter and the walk around the island is calmer too (more on crowds and timing in our best time to visit guide).

Dinner. Travellers who stay the night often notice the same thing: once the day visitors leave, many of the cheaper crêperies and shops on the island close, and mostly the hotel restaurants stay open. So it's best to book dinner ahead — or eat on the mainland.

Picnic. Another option is to buy food on the mainland and eat outdoors with a view of the bay. Take your rubbish with you, of course.

Practical tips

  • Book dinner on the island ahead, especially in season.
  • Eat before or after the lunchtime peak to avoid queues.
  • Check the Mère Poulard omelette price on the current menu, not in old reviews.
  • Look for local AOP produce on menus: salt-meadow lamb and mussels from the bay.
  • Bring water: the climb to the abbey is long and steep.
  • If you're staying on the mainland, dinner there is often easier and calmer.

Frequently asked questions

How much is the Mère Poulard omelette?

It depends on the format and changes over time; sources range from several tens of euros for an omelette to more for a tasting menu. Check the restaurant's current menu.

Is the Mère Poulard omelette worth it?

If you're interested in the history and the show, yes, as a one-off experience. If you just want a filling, inexpensive lunch, a crêperie is the simpler choice.

Where is the cheapest place to eat at Mont-Saint-Michel?

Usually the crêperies on the Grande Rue, or the mainland by the car park. Another option is a picnic with food bought on the mainland.

Can I have dinner on the island in the evening?

Yes, but there's less choice than during the day: many inexpensive places close earlier. It's best to book dinner ahead or eat on the mainland.

Which local dishes should I try?

Galettes and crêpes, salt-meadow lamb (AOP Prés-salés du Mont-Saint-Michel) and mussels from the bay (AOP), plus Mère Poulard biscuits as a souvenir.

The takeaway

You can eat well at Mont-Saint-Michel if you know what to expect: the Mère Poulard omelette is history and spectacle at its own price, the crêperies are a simple lunch, and local lamb and mussels are the real taste of the bay. Eat outside the peak, book dinner ahead or eat on the mainland — and give the time between meals to the island itself, at your own pace and with all its stories.
